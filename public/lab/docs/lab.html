How MEDUSA Lab works

Where your data lives, where the Python runs, and exactly how the engine turns candles and rules into trades.

The pieces

  • Data comes from Binance, straight to your browser. Save it in MEDUSA’s Downloads folder, or as a CSV/ZIP file on your computer that you can upload later (it then goes to the Uploads folder). Files on MEDUSA are compressed in your browser first (about 8–15 bytes per candle, prices kept exactly). See .
  • Results of every backtest are saved in the Results folder, and can be exported as CSV. The Files tab shows all three folders, lets you delete files one by one or a whole folder, and shows how much of Cloudflare’s free storage is left (also in the Lab’s top bar).
  • Backtests run in your browser. MEDUSA runs on Cloudflare’s free plan, which allows only a few milliseconds of computing per request, too little for a backtest. Your browser has plenty, so the Python engine runs there. The first start downloads about 16 MB (Python and numpy, kept by the browser after that) and takes a few seconds.
  • Strategies are Python files. The built-in ones are in MEDUSA’s public/lab/strategies folder; yours are saved in your account (Strategies tab).
  • The Python tab runs any code you write on your data files, with numpy, pandas, matplotlib, scipy and statsmodels, and the same backtest engine. See .
  • Every report has a “Python code” button: one standalone file with the engine, the strategy and the exact settings. Run it with Python 3 and numpy on any computer and it downloads the same candles from Binance (or reads your file), repeats the test and checks that it gets the same numbers.
  • Times are shown in your time zone, including the trade list, the charts and the monthly table (months and days are your local ones). Strategy rules that name a time, such as “the 21:00 UTC candle”, are defined in UTC like the exchange’s candles; the guide shows your local time next to them. Exported results are in your time too, with the zone in the column name (for example “Entry time (UTC+05:30)”); candle data files keep Binance’s UTC times.
  • ₹ INR (top right) shows money amounts and USDT prices in rupees, at CoinDCX’s current USDT/INR price. Percentages and R multiples don’t change. The backtest itself always runs in USDT, and files you download or export stay in USDT.
  • Saving is safe to repeat. Saving the same download, upload, result or strategy again finds the copy already in your folders instead of storing it twice.

How each candle is processed

The engine goes through the candles in order. For candle i:

  1. At its open: market orders placed at the previous close are filled at this open (exits first, then entries).
  2. Inside the candle: waiting stop/limit entries are checked against the high and low, then the open position’s stop and target. A candle doesn’t show the path the price took, so the engine assumes the worse case: if one candle touches both the stop and the target, the stop counts first; a position entered inside a candle can be stopped in that candle but can’t reach its target there; a candle that opens beyond the stop fills at that open (a gap), not at the stop.
  3. Funding (futures) settled during the candle is paid or received if a position is still open, at the real historical rate.
  4. Time exits: a position held for its maximum number of candles is closed at this candle’s close.
  5. At its close: your strategy’s on_bar(i) sees candles 0 to i and may place orders for the next candle.

With “Market orders fill at: Signal candle’s close” in the settings, step 5’s market orders fill at that close instead of the next open. That is slightly optimistic (you can’t really trade at a close you only see once it’s final), so the default is the next open.

Warm-up: the history indicators need

Indicators need past candles. A 200-candle average has no value until 200 candles exist. Averages that start from the first price (EMA, RSI, ATR) have a value at once, but it still depends on where they started and only settles after a few times their length. Trading on an unsettled indicator would give different trades from someone who had the chart open all along, so MEDUSA loads enough history first.

  • How much: each length setting says what it feeds. A plain window (an average, the highest high, a channel) needs 1× its length, an EMA 4×, Wilder’s smoothing (RSI, ATR, ADX) 8×; the strategy needs the largest of these, plus one candle. A 21-candle EMA needs 85 candles, a 14-candle ATR 113, MACD 12/26/9 141. The backtest form shows the warm-up for the settings you typed, in candles and in time, and updates as you change them (a 35-day EMA on 1-hour candles needs 3,361 candles, 140 days).
  • With a start date, those candles are taken from before the start date. They are only used for the calculations: trading, the account and every number in the report start at the start date. This is what a trader who had the chart open would have seen that day. If the file doesn’t go back far enough, the test starts later, as soon as the warm-up is complete, and the report says so.
  • Without a start date, the first candles of the file are the warm-up, and the test starts right after them. Download a little more history than you want to test if that matters.
  • The report’s settings list shows how many warm-up candles were used. The same settings always give the same warm-up, so a test can be repeated exactly (also with the exported Python code).

Costs

  • Fee per fill, as a share of the traded value. The default 0.05% is Binance’s futures taker fee; a round trip pays it twice.
  • Slippage per fill: every fill price is moved against you by this share (buy higher, sell lower). The default 0.05% is a cautious guess for liquid coins; small coins slip more.
  • Funding on futures, from the real history (Binance datasets imported with funding rates). Longs usually pay shorts in bull markets, which adds up for positions held for days or weeks.

Every report also re-runs the same test without any costs and shows both, so you can see how much of a result the costs eat.

Position size and leverage

  • Risk a % per trade (default 1%): the position is sized so that hitting the first stop loses that share of the account. A stop twice as far away means a position half as big. Strategies without a stop fall back to the other method.
  • A % of the account: the position’s value is a fixed share of the account (100% = 1x, 200% = 2x).
  • Max leverage caps either method: the position is never worth more than this many times the account. With tight stops, risk-based sizing often hits this cap.
  • Compounding (default on) sizes from the current balance; off sizes from the starting money.
  • Liquidation is simulated like Binance’s cross margin: if the account falls to the maintenance margin (0.5% of the position’s value), the position is closed and the rest is lost. Once the account is empty, no new trades open.

Spot and futures

Futures datasets can trade long and short with leverage, and pay funding. Spot datasets only take long trades, never use more than 1x, and pay no funding; the report says so when it changed your settings.

Safety and privacy

  • Your data files, results and strategy files belong to your account only.
  • Strategy code (including files you upload) runs inside your browser’s Python engine, in a background thread whose network access is switched off once Python has loaded: it can calculate, but it can’t send anything anywhere or reach your account.
