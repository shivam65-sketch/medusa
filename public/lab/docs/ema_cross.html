EMA crossover

Buy when a fast moving average crosses above a slow one, sell short when it crosses below. It is the classic way to try to catch the start of a trend, and these are the exact rules MEDUSA’s demo trading bot uses.

TypeShort-term trend following
TradesLong and short (long only on spot)
EntryEMA 9 crosses EMA 21, at the next candle’s open
ExitStop 2 × ATR, target 4 × ATR (= 2R), or after 24 candles

The idea

A moving average smooths the price so you can see its direction. A fast average (few candles) follows the price closely; a slow one (more candles) lags behind. When the price starts rising, the fast average turns up first and crosses above the slow one. The strategy treats that moment as “a new up-trend may be starting” and buys; the mirror image (fast crosses below slow) is a short.

The weakness is built in: averages lag, so the cross comes after part of the move, and in a sideways market the two lines cross back and forth, producing a string of small losses (“whipsaws”). The fixed target and the time limit keep trades short, so the strategy needs many small wins to pay for those.

The ingredients

EMA: exponential moving average

Each new value moves part of the way from the previous average towards the latest close:

k = 2 / (n + 1)                    n = 9  →  k = 0.2
EMA today = close × k + EMA yesterday × (1 − k)

Example with n = 9: yesterday’s EMA was 100 and today’s close is 110. Today’s EMA = 110 × 0.2 + 100 × 0.8 = 102. A jump in price moves the 9-EMA a fifth of the way, and the 21-EMA (k ≈ 0.09) less than a tenth of the way, which is why the fast one crosses first.

ATR: average true range

The true range of a candle is its high minus its low, stretched to include any gap from the previous close. ATR(14) is its average over 14 candles (smoothed with an EMA here, like the bot). If BTC’s 4-hour candles typically span $700, ATR ≈ 700, and a stop 2 × ATR away sits $1,400 from the entry: far enough that normal wiggles don’t hit it, close enough to cap the loss. In quiet markets the stop tightens automatically; in wild ones it widens.

The rules, exactly

  1. At each candle’s close, compute EMA(9) and EMA(21) of the closes, and ATR(14).
  2. Long: if EMA 9 is above EMA 21 now, and was at or below it one candle earlier, buy at the next candle’s open.
  3. Short: the mirror image (EMA 9 crosses below EMA 21).
  4. Stop: 2 × ATR from the entry price. Target: 4 × ATR from the entry (twice the stop distance, so a win is +2R before costs).
  5. Time exit: if neither is hit after 24 candles, close at that candle’s close.
  6. Only one trade at a time; crosses while a trade is open are ignored, unless “Also exit on the opposite cross” is on: then the trade is closed and one in the new direction opened, at the same open.

When it works, and when it doesn’t

  • Works: markets that make clean, sustained moves after a turn, on timeframes where each move is large compared with trading costs.
  • Fails: sideways, choppy markets (the averages keep crossing) and sharp V-shaped reversals (the cross comes after the move is over).

The average trade is in % of the position; t above 2 means it is unlikely to be luck. “Profitable after costs” needs a positive average with t ≥ 2 and a profit on most coins; “Edge before costs only” means the rule predicts something, but less than the trading costs.

Earlier tests

BTC 4h, Sep 2019 – Oct 2026 (trading starts after the warm-up), default settings, with fees, slippage and real funding: 425 trades, 44% winners, +9.1% in seven years (buy and hold: +741%), worst drawdown 28.6%. Before costs the same trades made +48.7%. The average trade was +0.03R with t = 0.47: no evidence of an edge.

Earlier research on the demo bot’s settings (1-hour candles, BTC, ETH, SOL, BNB and PEOPLE): 8,090 trades lost −546R after costs, although they made +395R before costs. Costs were about 0.12R per trade, more than the signal’s edge, and all nine neighbouring EMA pairs (7–11 against 17–25) lost too.

So the crossover has a small edge before costs that the costs more than eat on short timeframes. Treat it as a learning example and a starting point, not a money-maker.

Ideas to explore

  • Test it on 1d candles, where each trend move is much larger than the costs.
  • Set the target to 0, turn on “exit on the opposite cross” and the time exit to 0: a pure trend follower that lets winners run.
  • Long only, and in your own copy, only buy when the price is above its 200-candle EMA (a trend filter).
  • Whatever you try, set a held-back date first and judge only the first part while tuning.

Common mistakes

  • Tuning the two lengths until the backtest looks great: with only two numbers to turn you can still fit the past. Check the neighbours of your favourite setting; if they are much worse, the favourite is luck.
  • Ignoring costs: on 1-minute to 1-hour candles they dominate this strategy.
