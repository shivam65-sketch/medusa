Price vs moving average (with a band)

The textbook academic trading rule: be long while the price is more than 1% above its 50-candle average, short while more than 1% below. Tested on a century of stock data, and on high-frequency Bitcoin data, where it did better than any other classic rule.

The idea, and where it comes from

Brock, Lakonishok and LeBaron tested simple moving-average rules on 90 years of the Dow Jones and found they predicted returns. Their “variable-length moving average” rule compares a short average (1 = the price itself) with a long one, and acts only when the gap is bigger than a band, to skip whipsaws. Corbet and co-authors found that rule worked best of the classic rules on high-frequency Bitcoin data; Hudson and Urquhart tested almost 15,000 technical rules on cryptocurrencies and found significant predictability.

The rules, exactly

  1. Ratio = short average (1 candle = the close) ÷ 50-candle simple average − 1.
  2. Ratio above +1% → be long; below −1% → be short; in between → keep the current position.
  3. A move to the other side closes and reverses the position at the next open. No stop.

Settings unchanged from the source. Binance USD-M futures for BTC, ETH, SOL, XRP, BNB, DOGE and PEPE (5-minute tests: BTC and ETH) from 2019, or each coin’s listing, to 2 Oct 2026. Lab default costs: 0.05% fee and 0.05% slippage on every fill, about 0.2% a round trip, plus real funding. The average trade is in % of the position; t above 2 means it is unlikely to be luck. “Profitable after costs” needs a positive average with t ≥ 2 and a profit on most coins; “Edge before costs only” means the rule predicts something, but less than the trading costs.

The papers were right that there is something here: on 1-hour candles the trades made a clear profit before costs. But costs took nearly all of it, and on 5-minute candles, the closest to the Bitcoin study, there was nothing left at all. In both papers trading costs were small or left out.
