Reading the report

Every backtest ends in the same report. Read it top to bottom: warnings first, then whether the result survives costs and the held-back period, and only then the headline profit.

1. “Read this first”

The yellow box lists what could make this result misleading: too few trades, an average trade that isn’t clearly above zero, a strategy that only works before costs, a huge drawdown, a liquidation, or settings you changed for spot. If something here applies, the rest of the report matters less.

2. The headline numbers

TileWhat it meansHow to judge it
Net profitMoney made or lost over the whole test after fees, slippage and funding.Compare it with buy and hold and with the drawdown it took to get there.
Yearly growth (CAGR)The steady yearly rate that turns the starting money into the final balance.Lets you compare tests of different lengths.
Worst drawdownThe deepest fall of the account from a high to a later low, and the longest time it stayed below a previous high.Ask honestly: would I keep trading through this? Most people quit at a fraction of what they think they can stand.
TradesHow many trades closed (long / short).Under 30 says almost nothing; 100+ starts to mean something.
Win rateThe share of trades that made money.Meaningless alone. Trend strategies win 30–40% and can be profitable; mean-reversion strategies win 55–70% and can still lose.
Profit factorAll money won on winners ÷ all money lost on losers.Below 1 loses. 1.0–1.2 after costs is fragile; 1.3+ is good; above 2 in a backtest is suspicious.
Average tradeThe average result per trade in R (1R = the first stop’s loss), with its t-statistic.The most honest single number. +0.1R or more after costs with t ≥ 2 is the bar to clear.
Sharpe ratioAverage daily return ÷ how much daily returns swing, scaled to a year (Sortino only counts the down days).Above 1 is good; above 2 in a backtest usually means something is too good to be true.

The t-statistic, simply. If a coin-flip strategy were traded 400 times, its average trade would wander a little above or below zero by luck. The t number says how many “luck-sized steps” away from zero your average is. Around 2 or more means luck is an unlikely explanation; under 1 means it could easily be luck. It says nothing about the future if the rules were tuned on this same data, which is why the held-back check matters.

3. Account value

Green: your account under the strategy. Grey: putting the same money into the coin on day one and holding it, with no fees or funding. The red area below shows how far the account was under its last high at each moment.

Look at the shape, not just the end: a line that rises in one burst and drifts for years is very different from a steady climb. Long flat or falling stretches are what living with the strategy feels like.

4. Price chart with every trade

▲ marks a long entry, ▼ a short entry, ● the exit (green = profit, red = loss). Click any row in the trade list (or use Previous / Next trade) to zoom to that trade with its stop and target drawn. Look at a few losing trades: do they fail for the reason you would expect?

5. Before and after costs

The same test, run once with no fees, slippage or funding. The gap is what trading costs you. A strategy that is profitable before costs but not after is common, especially on short timeframes: every trade pays about 0.2% for the round trip (fee and slippage, in and out), which can be bigger than the move a 1-minute strategy aims for.

Example from testing the Lab: the Turtle breakout on BTCUSDT 1-minute candles for one year made 27,545 trades and lost the whole account. Its stops were so tight that the round-trip cost of each trade was about two times what the stop risked, so the average trade lost −3.5R. The very same rules on 4-hour candles made money. Timeframe and costs are part of the strategy.

6. Held-back check

Appears when you set a held-back date. The report splits the trades into “before” and “from” that date. The idea: choose and tune settings looking only at the “before” part, and treat the “from” part as a preview of the future. If “before” is great and “from” is flat or negative, the settings were probably fitted to the past.

It only works if you don’t peek: once you change settings because of the held-back result, that period is no longer held back.

7. By year, long and short, how trades ended

  • By year: is the profit spread over several years, or did one year make all of it?
  • Long and short: in a rising market, shorts often lose. A strategy that only makes money one way may really be a bet on the market’s direction.
  • How trades ended: stops, targets, time exits and signal exits, with their average result. For example, if time exits lose on average, the trades may need longer, or the idea is weaker than it looks.

8. Monthly returns

Each cell is the account’s change in that month, counted in your time zone (green up, red down, stronger colour = bigger). Runs of red months show the stretches you would have to sit through.

9. Spread of trade results

Trades counted by result in R. Typical shapes:

  • Trend following (Turtle, EMA cross): a tall bar near −1R (many small losses at the stop) and a long tail to the right (a few big winners that pay for everything).
  • Mean reversion (RSI, Bollinger): many small winners and a left tail of full-stop losers. Watch for rare very large losses.

10. All trades and settings

The full list with entry and exit times (in your time zone), prices, the exit reason, candles held, costs, profit, R and return. The settings table at the end records exactly what was tested, including the strategy’s settings and the warm-up candles used, so a saved result always explains itself.

Saving, exporting and deleting results

  • Every backtest is saved automatically in the Results folder on MEDUSA (untick “Save the result in the Results folder” to skip it). Running exactly the same test again doesn’t make a second copy. The Dashboard lists them above the report; click one to show it.
  • Export CSV downloads the whole report as a CSV file for Excel or Google Sheets: the settings, the warnings, a summary table (after costs, before costs and buy and hold side by side), the held-back check, by year, monthly returns, long and short, how trades ended, the spread of results, every trade, and the account value over time.
  • Python code shows the complete standalone Python program that repeats this test on any computer (engine, strategy, settings, data download), with Copy and Download. See .
  • Use these settings puts the test’s exact settings back in the form, to change one thing and run it again.
  • Delete removes a result from MEDUSA; the Files tab can also empty the whole Results folder. A saved result keeps every number and table; only its price chart needs the data file, so it shows a note if that file was deleted.
