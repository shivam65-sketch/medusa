VWAP trend

Be long while the price is above the day’s volume-weighted average price, short while it is below, and flat when the day ends. The simplest intraday trend-following rule there is, made famous by a 2023 paper that claimed huge returns on US stocks.

TypeIntraday trend following
Candles1 to 15 minutes
TradesLong and short, many a day, always flat at the session’s end
SourceZarattini & Aziz, “VWAP: The Holy Grail for Day Trading Systems” (2023)

The idea, and where it comes from

The VWAP is the average price paid for every coin traded so far today, each trade weighted by its size. Big funds use it as their yardstick, so a price above the VWAP means today’s buyers are in profit and in control; below it, the sellers are. Carlo Zarattini and Andrew Aziz tested the bluntest possible use of that on the Nasdaq-100 fund QQQ with 1-minute candles: long above the VWAP, short below, reverse on every cross. They reported a 671% return from 2018 to 2023 with a 9.4% worst drawdown.

Crypto has no opening bell, so here a session is a 24-hour day starting at 00:00 UTC (you can move it, for example to 13:30 UTC, the US stock market open).

The rules, exactly

  1. The VWAP restarts at each session’s start: typical price (high + low + close) ÷ 3, weighted by volume.
  2. From the session’s second candle on, at each close: above the VWAP → be long; below → be short. Orders fill at the next open.
  3. On every cross, the position is closed and reversed.
  4. At the session’s last candle the position is closed (fills at the next session’s first open).
  5. No stop and no target in the original; the opposite cross is the exit.

A winning trade

When the price trends away from the VWAP and stays on one side, the position rides the whole move.

A losing trade

Most of the time the price wanders around the VWAP: every cross flips the position for a small loss plus costs.

Lab default costs: 0.05% fee and 0.05% slippage on every fill, about 0.2% a round trip, plus real funding. The average trade is in % of the position; t above 2 means it is unlikely to be luck. “Profitable after costs” needs a positive average with t ≥ 2 and a profit on most coins; “Edge before costs only” means the rule predicts something, but less than the trading costs.

The rule really does have a small edge before costs (a few hundredths of a percent a trade, very consistent), but it trades so often that the fees and slippage are about ten times bigger. Every account went to nearly zero. On QQQ the paper’s trades were cheap; on crypto futures each round trip costs about 0.2%.

Ideas to explore

  • Set fee and slippage to what you would really pay (maker fees on Binance are 0.02%) and see where the edge disappears.
  • Use a buffer of 0.1–0.3% or the 13:30 UTC session start to trade far less often.
